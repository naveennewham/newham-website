A purchase order (PO) approval process decides who can commit the company's money, up to what amount, and what has to be checked before a supplier is paid. In a construction company it protects two things at once: the project budget and the company's cash. Done well it is quick for site teams and hard to get around. Done badly it either slows every order or becomes a formality that nobody follows.

This guide sets out a process that suits Singapore contractors: from the request on site to the payment, with an approval matrix you can adapt. For tracking the spend against the budget once it is committed, see [budget vs actual in construction](/resources/budget-vs-actual-construction).

## The process at a glance

| Step | Who | What is checked |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Purchase request | Site engineer, supervisor or PM | The item is needed, and which project and budget line it belongs to |
| 2. Quotes | Buyer or PM | Enough quotes for the value, compared on the same basis |
| 3. Approval | PM, then managers by limit | The amount is within budget and within the approver's limit |
| 4. Purchase order | Buyer | Supplier, items, rates, delivery date and terms match the approved request |
| 5. Delivery | Site team | What arrived, in what quantity and condition |
| 6. Invoice match | Accounts payable | Invoice against PO and delivery: quantity, rate and GST details |
| 7. Payment approval | Managers by limit | The match is clean, and any difference is explained |
| 8. Payment | Finance | Paid by the due date, and the PO closed when complete |

Each step has one owner. The process fails at the hand-offs, so make each hand-off a record, not a message in a chat group.

## Step 1: The purchase request

Most problems start here. A good request states:

- **Project and budget line**: which project and which workhead or cost code the cost will land on
- **What and how much**: item description, specification or brand, quantity and unit
- **When**: the date it is needed on site, so the buyer knows how much time there is to get quotes
- **Why**: a short reason, especially for anything not in the original budget

Requests that cannot be tied to a budget line should not move forward until someone decides where the cost belongs. That one rule does more for cost control than any report.

## Step 2: Quotes and comparison

Set the number of quotes by value, for example one quote for small orders, two or three for larger ones, and a formal comparison for major packages. When you compare, put the quotes on the same basis: same scope, same quantities, delivery included or excluded, GST shown separately. The cheapest quote on paper is often the one that leaves out delivery, testing or a minimum order charge.

Keep the comparison with the request. When someone asks six months later why a supplier was chosen, the answer should be on file.

## Step 3: Approval limits by role

An approval matrix sets who approves what. The figures below are only an example; set your own amounts to suit the size of your projects and your appetite for risk.

| Value of request (SGD, before GST) | Approved by |
|---|---|
| Up to 5,000 | Project manager |
| 5,000 to 50,000 | Project manager, then general manager |
| Above 50,000 | Project manager, general manager, then managing director |
| Any amount outside the budget | As above, plus whoever approves budget changes |

Three rules make the matrix work:

1. **The requester never approves their own request.** This is the simplest control against error and fraud.
2. **Splitting is not allowed.** Two orders for 4,900 dollars to the same supplier on the same day is one order for 9,800 dollars.
3. **Changes restart approval.** If the amount or the supplier changes after approval, the request goes back through the matrix.

## Step 4: Issuing the purchase order

The buyer turns the approved request into a PO. The PO is the contract with the supplier, so it should state the items, quantities, rates, delivery address and date, payment terms and your PO number, which the supplier must quote on the delivery order and the invoice. If a PO is amended, issue a new revision rather than editing the old one, so everyone can see what changed.

Occasionally a PO is raised without a request, for example a framework order with a regular supplier. Allow it only with a written justification.

## Step 5: Delivery and goods received

When goods arrive, the site team checks them against the PO and the delivery order: quantity, specification and condition. The site endorses what was actually received, which may be less than what was ordered or what is on the delivery order. Short deliveries and rejected items are recorded on the spot. This endorsement is what accounts will rely on before paying.

## Step 6: Three-way invoice matching

Accounts payable matches each invoice against:

- **The PO**: is the rate the agreed rate, and is the item on the order?
- **The delivery endorsement**: is the quantity billed no more than the quantity received?
- **The request**: does the cost land on the right project and budget line?

Also check the invoice itself. For GST-registered purchases, IRAS requires a valid tax invoice addressed to your company before you can claim the GST as input tax. A missing GST registration number or a wrong company name means asking the supplier to reissue it. Block invoice numbers you have already recorded from the same supplier, so nothing is paid twice.

Anything that does not match is a discrepancy. It goes back to the buyer or PM to resolve with the supplier before payment, not after.

## Step 7: Payment approval and payment

Payment approval follows its own matrix, often the PM, then a project director, then a director, with each step taken by a different person. The approvers should see the match result, not just the invoice. A payment should never exceed what is left to pay on the invoice after credit notes, advances and earlier payments.

Once the order is fully delivered and invoiced, close the PO so that nothing more can be billed against it.

## Subcontracts follow the same path

A subcontract is a purchase too, usually the largest one on the project. Raise it against the budget, approve it within the matrix and issue it with the same care as a PO. The difference is in steps 5 to 7: instead of a delivery order, each progress claim from the subcontractor is assessed against the work actually done. See [subcontractor progress claims](/resources/subcontractor-progress-claims-singapore) for how to assess and respond to them.

## Common mistakes

- **Approval by chat message.** It is quick, but there is no record of who approved what, and no check against the budget.
- **Limits nobody remembers.** If the matrix is in a policy file that nobody reads, it is not a control. Build it into the system people use.
- **Paying from the delivery order.** The delivery order shows what the supplier says was sent, not what the site accepted.
- **POs that never close.** Open POs let late or duplicate invoices through months after the work.
- **Raising the PO after the invoice.** It records the cost but controls nothing.
