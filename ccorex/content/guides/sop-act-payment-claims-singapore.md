Under Singapore's Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act 2004 (the SOP Act), a contractor gets paid through two documents. The claimant serves a **payment claim**. The respondent answers with a **payment response** that states how much it will pay and why it is withholding any balance. Both documents must meet the Act's requirements and deadlines. If they do not, the claimant may apply for adjudication, and the respondent's chance to argue its position can be cut down.

This guide covers those two documents in detail: what goes in them, when they are due, and what happens when the response is late, missing or disputed. For the wider picture of how a monthly claim is valued, see our [progress claims guide](/resources/progress-claims-singapore).

All timelines below come from BCA's SOP Information Kit (second edition, June 2020), which reflects the amendments that came into force on 15 December 2019. BCA counts "days" as calendar days excluding official public holidays. Always check the current Act on [Singapore Statutes Online](https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/BCISPA2004) and [BCA's Security of Payment page](https://www1.bca.gov.sg/regulatory-info/security-of-payment) before acting.

## Who the Act covers

The Act gives a statutory right to progress payment to anyone who carries out construction work, or supplies related goods or services, under a written contract. It applies to public and private projects, and down the chain: main contractor to owner, subcontractor to main contractor, sub-subcontractor to subcontractor, and suppliers and consultants.

Some points to check before you rely on it:

- **Written contract.** The Act applies to written contracts, or the written part of a contract.
- **Some residential work is excluded.** Work on residential property that does not need building plan approval under the Building Control Act, such as most home renovation, is outside the Act.
- **Pure trading is excluded.** A supply contract is covered only where the goods are for a specified project or site.
- **Employees cannot claim.** Employment contracts are outside the Act.
- **Pay-when-paid clauses are void.** A main contractor cannot refuse to pay a subcontractor just because it has not been paid by the owner.

## The payment claim

### When to serve it

A payment claim is served on the date, or by the last day of the period, stated in the contract or agreed in writing. If the contract says nothing, the default is that a claim may be served by the last day of each month following the month in which the contract was made. Either way, a claimant must not serve more than one payment claim in each month.

Since the 2019 amendments, for contracts made after they came into force:

1. **Early service is allowed**, but an early claim is treated as served on the last day for service under the contract. The respondent's clock starts from that contractual date, not from when you sent it.
2. **Unpaid amounts can be claimed again.** An amount from an earlier claim that was not paid and not adjudicated can be included in a later claim.
3. **There is a time limit.** BCA states that, generally, a claim cannot be made more than 30 months after the work it covers was last carried out, or the goods or services were last supplied. For construction contracts, the slides on the amendments explain that the 30 months run from the latest of a few events, including the last certificate of completion. Check the Act for the exact wording.

### What it must contain

According to BCA, a payment claim must:

1. Be in writing and served on the respondent, or on anyone the contract names for this purpose
2. Identify the contract the progress payment relates to
3. State the claimed amount, calculated by reference to the period the claim covers
4. Give enough detail to identify the items claimed, such as type of work, goods or services, and the quantity of each
5. Be supported by documents and calculations, worked out under the contract or on a reasonable-value basis

A pure supplier may use its invoice as the payment claim if it contains these details.

### Does it need to say "made under the SOP Act"?

Not in Singapore. BCA's Information Kit says the claimant can highlight that the claim is made under the Act, "although this is not necessary". Stating it is still good practice: it tells the respondent the statutory clock is running and removes any argument that the document was only a draft valuation.

A simple line at the top works: "This is a payment claim made under the Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act 2004."

### How to serve it

Serve the claim by a method the Act or the contract allows. Since 2019 the Act allows personal delivery, leaving it at the usual place of business during business hours, post, fax, email, and an instant messaging platform the parties have agreed to, in an agreed file format. BCA's amendment notes say other methods are not permitted.

Keep proof of service every time.

## The payment response

### What it must contain

A payment response is the respondent's written answer to the claim. BCA lists that it must:

1. Be in writing and addressed to the claimant
2. Identify the payment claim it relates to
3. State the amount the respondent proposes to pay, or "nil" if it proposes to pay nothing
4. Give all reasons where the response amount is less than the claim
5. Break down the items behind each reason
6. Show the calculations behind the response amount

A payment certificate from the architect or superintending officer can count as a payment response, provided it meets these requirements.

### When it is due

For construction contracts, a payment response is required in every case, even if the respondent plans to pay in full. It must be given by the date in the contract, subject to a maximum of 21 days after the date (or last day of the period) when the claim is required to be served. If the contract is silent, the default is 14 days. The 2019 amendments raised this default from 7 days.

Supply contracts are different. A payment response is not required, but a respondent who withholds any amount must give written reasons by the payment due date.

### Raise every reason now

This is the most important rule for respondents. For construction contracts, reasons for withholding that are not in the payment response cannot be raised in the adjudication response, and since 2019 generally cannot be used to set aside the determination in court either. BCA summarises this as "speak now or forever hold your peace". There are limited exceptions, such as reasons that could not have been known when the response was given.

Damage, loss and expense items are also restricted. Since 2019 an adjudicator only considers them if they are supported by a written agreement between the parties or a certificate issued under the contract.

### Accepting or disputing the response

If the claimant accepts the response, it should say so in writing, typically by issuing its invoice (a tax invoice if GST-registered). If it does not accept in writing, it is treated as disputing the response.

## After the response: payment and adjudication

### Payment due date

For construction contracts, payment is due by the date in the contract, subject to a maximum of 35 days. If the contract is silent, the default is 14 days. BCA's flowchart counts these periods from the payment response due date, or from when the tax invoice is provided where one is needed. For pure supply contracts, the maximum is 60 days and the default 30 days after the claim (invoice) is served.

Interest runs on late payments at the higher of the contract rate and the judgment debt rate.

### Dispute settlement period

If no response arrives on time, or the claimant disputes it, a 7-day dispute settlement period follows the response due date. The parties can try to settle, and the respondent can give or vary its response. The claimant cannot apply for adjudication during this period.

### Adjudication application

The claimant must first serve a notice of intention to apply for adjudication on the respondent. It then has 7 days to lodge the application with the Singapore Mediation Centre (SMC), the authorised nominating body. The 7 days run from:

- the end of the dispute settlement period, if there was no response or the response is disputed, or
- the payment due date, if the claimant accepted the response amount but was not paid.

Miss that window and the claimant loses the right to adjudicate that claim, though it can include the unpaid amount in its next claim.

SMC appoints an adjudicator within 7 days. The respondent may lodge an adjudication response within 7 days of receiving the application. The adjudicator then decides within 7 days in simple cases (no responses at all, or an accepted amount not paid) or 14 days in other cases, unless both parties agree to more time. SMC accepts lodgments by hand or by email; check its [adjudication page](https://mediation.com.sg/our-services/overview-of-services/adjudication-2/) for current procedures and fees.

## Summary of key periods

| Step | Contract may set | Default if contract silent |
|---|---|---|
| Serve payment claim | Date or period in contract | By last day of each month after the month the contract was made |
| Payment response (construction) | Up to 21 days | 14 days |
| Dispute settlement period | Fixed by the Act | 7 days after response due date |
| Payment due (construction) | Up to 35 days | 14 days |
| Payment due (pure supply) | Up to 60 days | 30 days |
| Lodge adjudication application | Fixed by the Act | Within 7 days of the trigger |
| Adjudication response | Fixed by the Act | Within 7 days of receiving the application |

## Common mistakes

1. **Relying on the contract alone.** The Act caps some contract periods and overrides pay-when-paid clauses. Read both.
2. **No proof of service.** Use an allowed method and keep the record.
3. **Two claims in one month.** Only one payment claim may be made in each month.
4. **Thin claims.** Claims without item details, quantities and supporting calculations invite disputes.
5. **Skipping the payment response.** For construction contracts a response is always required, even for full payment.
6. **Incomplete reasons.** A respondent that leaves a reason out of the response may not be able to raise it later.
7. **Missing the 7-day window.** Adjudication deadlines are short. Diarise them on the day the claim is served.
8. **Forgetting to accept in writing.** If you agree with the response, confirm it with your invoice.
