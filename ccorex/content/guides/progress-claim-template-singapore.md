A progress claim template is the standard layout a contractor uses to claim payment each period. In Singapore it usually has three parts: a header that identifies the contract and claim, a valuation sheet that values every item to date, and a summary that adds variations, deducts retention and previous claims, and adds GST at 9% to arrive at the amount claimed this period.

This guide walks through that layout column by column, with a worked example. It focuses on the format. For how claims fit into the contract and the wider payment process, see our [progress claims guide](/resources/progress-claims-singapore). For the statutory requirements and deadlines, see [SOP Act payment claims and payment responses](/resources/sop-act-payment-claims-singapore).

## Part 1: The header

The header tells the reader exactly which contract and period the claim relates to. Under the SOP Act, BCA says a payment claim must identify the contract and state the claimed amount by reference to the period it covers, so the header is not just decoration.

| Field | Example | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Claimant | Your company name and UEN | Identifies who is claiming |
| Respondent | The party you contracted with | The claim must be served on the right party |
| Project | Project title and site | Ties the claim to the project |
| Contract / subcontract reference | Letter of award or PO number and date | Identifies the contract |
| Claim number | PC 07 | One running sequence per project |
| Claim period | Work up to 31 Oct | The period the valuation covers |
| Date of claim | Date served | Starts the response timeline |
| SOP Act statement | "This is a payment claim made under the Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act 2004." | Not legally required, but it removes doubt |

Number claims in one unbroken sequence per project. A skipped or repeated number is the first thing a quantity surveyor will query.

## Part 2: The valuation sheet

The valuation sheet values each line of the contract to date. It normally follows the priced BOQ or the contract's schedule of rates, so a clean BOQ makes this sheet much easier to build. See [what is a BOQ](/resources/what-is-a-boq) for how BOQs are structured.

### The columns

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Item | BOQ or schedule reference |
| Description | Short description of the work |
| Contract amount | The priced amount for the item from the contract |
| Qty or % to date | Measured quantity installed, or percentage complete |
| Value to date | Contract amount × % complete, or quantity × rate |
| Previously claimed | Value to date on the last claim |
| This period | Value to date − previously claimed |
| Remarks | Basis of assessment, drawing or photo reference |

Two valuation methods are common, and many claims use both:

- **Measured:** quantity installed × BOQ rate. Suits pipework, ductwork, cabling and devices.
- **Percentage complete:** each item assessed as a percentage. Suits equipment, lump-sum items and preliminaries.

The "Remarks" column is worth the effort. BCA states that a payment claim must give enough detail to identify the items and the quantity of each, and must be supported by documents and calculations. A short note pointing to a marked-up drawing, delivery order or site photo makes the claim easier to certify and harder to cut.

### Variations

Put variations on a separate sheet with the same columns plus two more:

| Extra column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| VO reference | The instruction or variation order number |
| Status | Instructed, submitted, agreed or disputed |

Only include variation work your contract allows you to claim. Show the status honestly. A claim that mixes unapproved variations into contract work tends to be reduced as a whole.

### Materials on site

Some contracts allow payment for materials delivered but not yet installed. If yours does, list them on their own sheet with delivery references. If it does not, leave them out.

## Part 3: The summary

The summary turns the valuation into the amount payable. Most contractors prepare it on a cumulative basis: value everything to date, then deduct what has already been certified.

### Template layout

| Line | Item | Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Contract sum | From the letter of award |
| 2 | Contract work done to date | Total of the valuation sheet |
| 3 | Variations done to date | Total of the variation sheet |
| 4 | Materials on site | Only if the contract allows |
| 5 | Gross value to date | 2 + 3 + 4 |
| 6 | Less retention | Contract rate on line 5, up to the contract limit |
| 7 | Net value to date | 5 − 6 |
| 8 | Less previous certified amounts | Total net certified on earlier claims (excluding GST) |
| 9 | Amount claimed this period | 7 − 8 |
| 10 | GST at 9% | 9 × 9%, if GST-registered |
| 11 | Total amount claimed this period | 9 + 10 |

Keep the contract sum (line 1) on the summary even though it is not part of the arithmetic. It lets the reader see at a glance how far through the job you are.

### Worked example

The figures below are illustrative only. Your contract sets the retention rate and limit. Here we assume retention of 5% of gross value, up to a limit of 5% of the contract sum.

| Line | Item | Amount (S$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Contract sum | 1,200,000.00 |
| 2 | Contract work done to date | 480,000.00 |
| 3 | Variations done to date | 35,000.00 |
| 4 | Materials on site | 0.00 |
| 5 | Gross value to date | 515,000.00 |
| 6 | Less retention (5% of line 5; limit 60,000) | (25,750.00) |
| 7 | Net value to date | 489,250.00 |
| 8 | Less previous certified amounts | (361,000.00) |
| 9 | Amount claimed this period | 128,250.00 |
| 10 | GST at 9% | 11,542.50 |
| 11 | Total amount claimed this period | 139,792.50 |

Note line 8: use the amounts **certified**, not the amounts you claimed. If last month's claim was cut, the difference comes back automatically in this month's cumulative value, so you do not need a separate "balance from last claim" line.

### GST

GST in Singapore is 9%. IRAS's guidance for the construction sector says GST on progress payments, and on retention when it is released, is accounted for at the earlier of receiving payment or issuing an invoice. IRAS also notes that a letter or statement of claim is generally not an invoice for this purpose. Agree with your accountant when you issue the tax invoice for each claim, and check [IRAS's construction page](https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/goods-services-tax-(gst)/specific-business-sectors/construction) for the current rules.

## Supporting documents to attach

1. Valuation sheet and variation sheet
2. Measurement records or marked-up drawings
3. Site photos with dates
4. Delivery orders for materials on site
5. Copies of variation instructions
6. The last certificate or payment response, to support line 8

## Common mistakes

1. **Not cumulative.** Claiming only "this month's work" makes errors hard to correct and the claim hard to reconcile.
2. **Using claimed instead of certified amounts.** Line 8 must match the certificates, or the claim will not reconcile.
3. **Retention past the limit.** Once retention reaches the contract limit, stop increasing it.
4. **Variations mixed in.** Keep them on their own sheet with references and status.
5. **GST on the wrong figure.** Apply GST to the amount for this period, not the gross value to date.
6. **No item detail.** A lump-sum figure with no breakdown is easy to dispute.
7. **Broken numbering.** Keep one claim sequence per project.
