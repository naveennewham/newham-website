Budget vs actual in construction means comparing what you planned to spend on each workhead with what you have committed and paid, while the project is still running. Done well, it tells a project manager which part of a job is overrunning in time to fix it. Done badly, it arrives at month-end as a surprise in the accounts.

This guide explains how to structure a project budget, how purchase request and PO approval connect to it, and what to look for in construction project management software in Singapore and India.

## Why budget vs actual goes wrong

Most contractors have a budget. The problem is that the budget lives in one place and the spending lives somewhere else.

- The estimate was priced in a tender spreadsheet.
- Purchases are approved by email or WhatsApp.
- Invoices go straight to accounts.
- Labour cost sits in payroll.
- Nobody joins them up until the project is nearly finished.

By the time the overrun is visible, the materials are installed and the subcontractor is paid. Cost control in construction is about moving the check earlier, to the point where someone asks to spend money.

## Step 1: Structure the budget so it can be tracked

A budget you cannot compare against is just a number. Break it down in the same way you will record spending.

### Workheads

Workheads are the trade or work packages: for an MEP contractor, things like sprinkler installation, ductwork, electrical containment, testing and commissioning. For a main contractor, they may be substructure, superstructure, architectural works and M&E.

### WBS

A work breakdown structure splits the project by area or phase: tower, podium, basement, or level by level. This lets you see whether one block is overrunning while others are fine.

### Cost centres

Cost centres group cost by type: materials, labour, subcontract, plant, preliminaries. They answer the question "what kind of cost is overrunning?"

A useful budget can be read along all three. The estimate you won the job with should map into this structure, which is why your estimating tool matters too. ## Step 2: Control spend at the point of request

This is where PO approval software earns its place. The control point is the purchase request, not the invoice.

### A controlled purchasing flow

1. **Purchase request (PR).** The site or project team raises a request against a specific budget line.
2. **Approval routing.** The request goes through the approvals your company has configured, for example project manager, then general manager, then managing director.
3. **Purchase order (PO).** Once approved, the PR becomes a PO to the supplier.
4. **Delivery.** Site confirms what actually arrived.
5. **Invoice matching.** The supplier's invoice is checked against the PO and the delivery.
6. **Payment.** Only matched invoices are paid.

Each approver should see the remaining budget on that line before approving. If the request takes the line over budget, that should be obvious, not buried.

### Checklist for PO approval software

- [ ] Every PR is linked to a project and a budget line
- [ ] Approvers see budget, committed and remaining amounts
- [ ] Approval routes are configurable by your company, not hard-coded
- [ ] Each approval is recorded with name and time
- [ ] POs are generated from approved PRs, not typed separately
- [ ] Deliveries can be recorded against the PO, including partial deliveries
- [ ] Invoices are matched against PO and delivery before payment
- [ ] Vendor and PO data can flow to your accounting system

## Step 3: Bring in labour and overheads

Materials and subcontracts are visible because they come with invoices. Labour and overheads are often missing from project cost, so margins look healthier than they are.

- **Site labour.** Timesheets record which project and workhead each worker spent time on. Attendance captured on site helps keep timesheets honest; see our guide to [construction payroll in Singapore](/resources/construction-payroll-singapore).
- **Overheads.** Office staff, vehicles and yard costs are real. Allocate them to projects at month-end on a basis you agree in advance and apply consistently.

## Step 4: Track revenue as well as cost

Budget vs actual is half the picture. You also need to see what you have claimed from the client.

- Record contract value, retention and payment terms for each project.
- Prepare progress claims against the contract, with retention deducted.
- In Singapore, apply GST (currently 9%; see [IRAS](https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/goods-services-tax-(gst))) on claims where applicable and issue a clear certificate.

Comparing claimed value with cost to date gives you a real view of project margin.

## Setting approval rules that people follow

Approval routes only work if they match how decisions are really made. Before you configure anything, agree the rules with the people who will approve.

- **Who approves what.** Decide which roles approve routine site materials, which approve subcontracts, and which approve anything that takes a budget line over its limit.
- **Value thresholds.** Many contractors send small requests to the project manager only and larger ones further up. Set thresholds that suit your business; there is no standard figure.
- **Over-budget requests.** Decide in advance whether an over-budget request is blocked, escalated or allowed with a reason. Write it down.
- **Urgent site purchases.** Sites will always need something today. Agree a fast route rather than letting urgency become an excuse to skip the system.
- **Delegation.** When an approver is on leave, someone else must be able to act, with the delegation recorded.

Keep the first version simple. It is easier to add a step later than to persuade people to use a route they find slow.

## What a good report looks like

The table below shows the columns a project manager should see for each workhead or cost centre.

| Column | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Budget | What you planned to spend |
| Committed | Approved POs and subcontracts not yet invoiced |
| Actual | Invoiced and paid cost, plus allocated labour and overhead |
| Committed + actual | Total cost you are already locked into |
| Remaining | Budget minus committed and actual |
| Variance | Where you are over or under, and by how much |

Review this weekly for active projects, not just at month-end. A short weekly review catches problems while you can still negotiate with a supplier or change a method.

## Choosing construction project management software in Singapore

"Construction project management software" covers everything from scheduling tools to document control. For cost control, focus on these questions:

- Does the budget structure (workhead, WBS, cost centre) match how you run projects?
- Can purchases only be raised against an approved budget?
- Can you configure your own approval routes?
- Does it handle the full chain from PR to payment?
- Can it produce client progress claims with retention and GST?
- Does it bring in labour from timesheets and allocate overhead?
- Does it link to your accounting software instead of replacing it?

## Common mistakes

- **Budget at the wrong level.** One line per project tells you nothing. Too many lines and nobody maintains it. Workheads plus cost centres is a sensible middle.
- **Ignoring committed cost.** If you only look at paid invoices, a large approved PO is invisible until the invoice arrives.
- **Approvals outside the system.** A "yes" on a phone message cannot be reported on. Keep approvals in one place.
- **No delivery check.** Paying invoices without confirming delivery invites short deliveries and duplicate billing.
- **Leaving out labour and overhead.** Project margins look fine until the company accounts disagree.
- **Rebuilding the budget by hand after award.** Retyping the estimate introduces errors. Bring it across from the tender.
- **Monthly-only reviews.** By month-end, the money is spent.
