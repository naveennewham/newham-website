IDD construction means running a project on connected digital tools and shared data from design through fabrication, construction and operation, instead of passing paper and re-typed spreadsheets between parties. In Singapore, it is driven by BCA's Integrated Digital Delivery programme, and regulatory submissions are moving to CORENET X. For contractors, the practical question is how your own estimating, cost and site records connect to that workflow.

This guide explains what IDD is, where adoption stands, what CORENET X changes, and how the commercial side of a contracting business fits in.

## What Integrated Digital Delivery means

IDD is BCA's approach to joining up the stages of a building project with digital tools. Typical elements include:

- **Digital design.** Building information models shared between architects, engineers and contractors.
- **Digital fabrication.** Prefabricated components produced from model data.
- **Digital construction.** Site progress, inspections and coordination tracked digitally.
- **Digital asset delivery and management.** Handover information that the building owner can use for operations.

The aim is that information created once is reused, not re-entered. Every time someone retypes a quantity from a drawing into a spreadsheet, or a site supervisor copies a paper form into a system, there is a chance of error and a delay.

## Where adoption stands

BCA's [Committee of Supply 2025 factsheet](https://www1.bca.gov.sg/docs/default-source/docs-corp-news-and-publications/media-releases/bca-media-factsheet---mnd-cos-2025.pdf) reports IDD adoption across new developments, measured by gross floor area:

| Year | IDD adoption (new developments, by GFA) |
|---|---|
| 2023 | about 58% |
| 2024 | about 67% |
| 2025 target | 70% |

In practice, this means most larger projects you tender for are likely to involve some form of IDD, and developers and main contractors will expect their supply chain to work digitally.

## CORENET X

CORENET X is Singapore's platform for regulatory submissions for building projects. It has been made mandatory in phases:

| From | Mandatory for |
|---|---|
| 1 October 2025 | New projects of 30,000 m² GFA or more ([URA circular DC25-07](https://www.ura.gov.sg/guidelines/circulars/dc25-07/)) |
| 1 October 2026 | New projects of 5,000 m² GFA or more ([URA circular DC26-08](https://www.ura.gov.sg/guidelines/circulars/dc26-08/)) |

The joint BCA and URA circular DC26-08, issued on 23 July 2026, updated the implementation plan. Earlier BCA material had scheduled CORENET X to become mandatory for **all** new projects, regardless of GFA, from 1 October 2026. Following industry feedback, the circular set the threshold at 5,000 m² instead. Under DC26-08:

- **Scope.** The requirement covers new buildings or works to existing buildings not yet formally submitted to a regulatory agency: new buildings of 5,000 m² GFA or more, additions or extensions adding 5,000 m² or more, and major addition and alteration works affecting or replacing 5,000 m² or more. It covers both the Three-Gateway Process and the Direct Submission Process.
- **Ongoing projects.** Projects of 5,000 m² or more that started submissions in CORENET 2 by 30 September 2026 may continue in CORENET 2 for now.
- **Smaller projects.** Projects below 5,000 m² can keep using CORENET 2 for now. Using the CORENET X Gateway Processes is voluntary for them, and the agencies say they will share their approach for this group later.

CORENET X is mainly about how design and regulatory information is submitted and approved. It is not a cost or estimating tool. But it reinforces the direction: project information is expected to be structured, digital and shared.

Read the circular itself for the exact scope, transition arrangements and any exceptions that may apply to your project.

## Where contractors fit

Much of the IDD discussion focuses on developers, consultants and main contractors managing models. Specialist contractors, including MEP and fire-protection firms, are part of the same chain. You receive drawings and models, produce shop drawings, order materials, install, test and hand over.

Your commercial records sit alongside that technical workflow:

- **Tender and estimate.** Quantities and prices for your scope.
- **Budget.** The estimate turned into a project budget by workhead.
- **Purchasing.** Purchase requests, orders, deliveries and invoices.
- **Progress claims.** What you have claimed from the client, with retention.
- **Labour.** Who worked on which project, and when.

If these live in disconnected spreadsheets and paper forms, you are the weak link in a digital workflow, even if your design team uses models.

## A practical checklist for contractors

You do not need to implement everything at once. Work through these in order.

### Information you receive

- [ ] Can your team open and use the drawings and models the main contractor shares?
- [ ] Do you have a clear process for tracking drawing revisions against your estimate?
- [ ] Is tender documentation stored in one place, not in personal inboxes?

### Information you produce

- [ ] Are estimates built from a shared component library, so quantities and rates can be traced?
- [ ] Can you export priced BOQs cleanly in the format the main contractor asks for?
- [ ] Are progress claims generated from project records, not typed afresh each month?

### Information you manage internally

- [ ] Is the project budget derived from the estimate, not rebuilt by hand?
- [ ] Are purchases raised against the budget and approved in one system?
- [ ] Is site attendance captured digitally and linked to projects?
- [ ] Can you see budget against actual at any time, not just at month-end?

## Connecting estimating to the rest of the project

The biggest gain for most specialist contractors is a clean link from estimate to budget to cost. When a tender is priced by area and workhead, that same structure can become the project budget after award. Purchases, labour and claims are then recorded against it.

This mirrors what IDD aims for on the design side: create information once and reuse it. Our guides on [choosing MEP estimating software](/resources/choosing-mep-estimating-software) and [budget vs actual in construction](/resources/budget-vs-actual-construction) cover each step in more detail.

## A phased approach for specialist contractors

Trying to digitalise every process at once usually stalls. A phased plan works better for most MEP and fire-protection firms.

### Phase 1: Tendering

Move estimating onto a shared component library with approvals. This gives you traceable quantities and rates, and it is the source for everything that follows.

### Phase 2: Project cost

After award, turn the estimate into a budget and require purchases to be raised against it. Record deliveries and match invoices before payment.

### Phase 3: Site and people

Capture attendance digitally at each site and link it to projects, so labour cost is visible alongside materials and subcontracts.

### Phase 4: Connect outward

Once your own records are digital, exchanging data with main contractors, consultants and their IDD platforms becomes a matter of export formats, not a clean-up exercise.

## What main contractors may ask of you

Requirements differ by project, but specialist contractors on IDD projects are commonly asked to:

- Work from shared models and coordinated drawings rather than standalone sets
- Submit shop drawings, method statements and inspection records digitally
- Provide quantities and progress in structured formats
- Hand over equipment and asset information for the building owner

None of these replace good commercial records. A contractor who can produce accurate quantities, a clear budget and traceable labour hours on request is easier to work with, and usually easier to pay on time, because claims are backed by data the main contractor can check.

Read the project's own IDD or BIM requirements in the tender documents and price the effort. It is real work, and it belongs in your estimate.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating IDD as only a BIM issue.** Models are part of it, but commercial and site data matter just as much to a contractor's margin.
- **Assuming CORENET X replaces your internal systems.** It handles regulatory submissions. Your estimating, purchasing and payroll still need their own tools.
- **Buying tools that cannot exchange data.** If every system needs manual re-entry, you have digitised the paper, not the workflow.
- **Leaving site records on paper.** Attendance and delivery records that start on paper delay claims and payroll.
- **Waiting for the main contractor to tell you what to use.** Get your own records in order first; it makes any project's digital requirements easier to meet.
- **Relying on second-hand summaries of the rules.** Read the BCA and URA sources directly, as requirements and thresholds are set there.
