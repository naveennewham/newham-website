A construction cost report shows, once a month, where each project's money has gone and where it is heading. It answers one question for the project manager and the directors: if we carry on as we are, what will this job finally cost us, and what margin will we make? Answering it while the project is running, not after the final account, is the whole point.

This guide explains each column of a standard monthly cost report, works through an example, and gives you an Excel template to start from. For setting up the budget and controlling spend before it reaches the report, see [budget vs actual in construction](/resources/budget-vs-actual-construction).

## The columns of a cost report

Each row is a cost code: a workhead, trade package or cost centre, as your budget is structured. Each column answers a different question.

| Column | Question it answers | Where the figure comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Budget | What did we plan to spend? | The approved budget, plus approved budget changes |
| Committed | What have we agreed to spend? | Approved POs and subcontracts, including amounts not yet invoiced |
| Actual to date | What have we spent? | Matched invoices, plus allocated labour and overhead |
| Cost to complete | What is still needed to finish? | The PM's estimate, at least the uninvoiced part of the commitments |
| Forecast final cost | What will this code finally cost? | Actual to date plus cost to complete |
| Variance | Are we over or under? | Budget minus forecast final cost |

Two columns do most of the work. **Committed** shows an overrun as soon as an order is placed, weeks before the invoice arrives. **Cost to complete** forces someone to think about the rest of the job instead of only reporting the past.

## A worked example

A fire protection subcontract worth SGD 1,200,000, three months in. Figures are in SGD before GST.

| Cost code | Budget | Committed | Actual to date | Cost to complete | Forecast final cost | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materials | 420,000 | 395,000 | 210,000 | 225,000 | 435,000 | (15,000) |
| Subcontract labour | 310,000 | 330,000 | 120,000 | 210,000 | 330,000 | (20,000) |
| Direct labour | 150,000 | | 60,000 | 85,000 | 145,000 | 5,000 |
| Equipment and testing | 45,000 | 18,000 | 10,000 | 30,000 | 40,000 | 5,000 |
| Preliminaries | 75,000 | 30,000 | 28,000 | 45,000 | 73,000 | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **1,000,000** | | **428,000** | **595,000** | **1,023,000** | **(23,000)** |

How to read it:

- **Subcontract labour is already over** budget on commitments alone: the subcontract was let at 330,000 against a budget of 310,000. That loss was locked in on the day the subcontract was signed. The report shows it now, not at the final invoice.
- **Materials are trending over.** Commitments are close to budget with more still to buy, so the PM's cost to complete pushes the forecast to 435,000. That points to a review of the remaining orders.
- **Direct labour, equipment and preliminaries are slightly under**, which partly offsets the overruns.

Against a contract value of 1,200,000, forecast margin has fallen from 200,000 at budget to 177,000. That is the number the directors need to see.

## Adding revenue: forecast margin

Put the revenue side at the top of the report:

- **Contract value**, plus **approved variations**
- **Claimed to date** and **certified to date**
- **Forecast final revenue**, including variations you expect to be agreed, shown separately from approved ones

Forecast margin is forecast final revenue minus forecast final cost. Track it month by month. A steady fall is an early warning even when no single cost code looks alarming.

## Preparing the report each month

1. **Close the month.** Post all matched invoices and allocate labour and overhead for the period, so actual to date is complete.
2. **Refresh commitments.** Include every approved PO and subcontract, and remove cancelled ones.
3. **Ask the PM for cost to complete.** Code by code, in figures, with a one-line reason for any change from last month.
4. **Review variances.** Anything over a set threshold gets an explanation and an action.
5. **Issue and keep it.** Date each month's report and keep the series. The trend matters as much as the latest figure.

## Common mistakes

- **Cost to complete set to "budget minus actual".** That always forecasts on budget and hides every problem.
- **Leaving out commitments.** Without them the report shows a healthy project until the invoices land.
- **No labour or overhead.** Invoices alone understate the true cost, especially on labour-heavy trades.
- **Different cost codes in budget and actuals.** If costs are coded differently from the budget, the variance column is meaningless.
- **Reporting cost without revenue.** Margin, not cost, is what tells you whether the job is going well.
