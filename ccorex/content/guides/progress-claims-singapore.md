A progress claim in Singapore is a contractor's periodic request for payment, usually monthly, valuing the work done to date under the contract, less retention and amounts already paid, plus GST where it applies. Payment claims in the construction industry are governed by the Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act (SOP Act), which sets out how claims are served, how the other party responds, and how disputes go to adjudication.

This guide covers how to prepare a progress claim, how retention works, how the SOP Act process fits together in general terms, and how to keep claims tied to your project costs. It is practical guidance, not legal advice. For current requirements and timelines, always check the Act and [BCA's Security of Payment guidance](https://www1.bca.gov.sg/regulatory-info/security-of-payment).

## What a progress claim does

Construction work is paid in stages because projects run for months or years. A progress claim:

- Values the work done so far against the contract
- Requests the amount due for the period
- Creates a record that can be checked, certified and, if needed, disputed
- Keeps cash moving so the contractor can pay suppliers, subcontractors and workers

The terms of your contract decide when you can claim, what you can claim for, and how claims are certified. The SOP Act sits alongside the contract and provides a statutory process for getting paid.

## How a progress claim is built up

Most claims are prepared on a cumulative basis: you value everything done to date, then subtract what has already been certified. This makes each claim self-correcting, because any under- or over-valuation last month is picked up this month.

| Line | What it means |
|---|---|
| A. Contract work done to date | Value of BOQ items completed, measured or assessed as a percentage |
| B. Approved variations to date | Value of variation work done, at agreed or assessed rates |
| C. Materials on site | Only if the contract allows payment for unfixed materials |
| D. Gross value to date | A + B + C |
| E. Less retention | Retention at the contract percentage, up to any limit in the contract |
| F. Net value to date | D − E |
| G. Less previously certified | Total of earlier certified amounts |
| H. Amount due this claim | F − G |
| I. GST | At the prevailing rate on H, if GST-registered |
| J. Total payable | H + I |

GST in Singapore is currently 9%; see [IRAS](https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/goods-services-tax-(gst)) for current rules. Confirm with your accountant how tax invoices should be issued against claims.

### Valuing the work

Value contract work against the priced BOQ. Two methods are common:

- **Measured.** Quantities installed are measured and multiplied by BOQ rates. Suits pipework, ductwork, cabling and devices.
- **Percentage complete.** Each item or section is assessed as a percentage complete. Suits equipment, lump-sum items and preliminaries.

Whichever method you use, keep the backup: marked-up drawings, site photos, delivery records, test reports. A claim without support is easy to reduce.

A clean, well-structured BOQ makes this much easier. See [what is a BOQ](/resources/what-is-a-boq) for how BOQs are laid out.

## Retention in construction

Retention is a part of each progress payment that the client holds back as security that the contractor will finish the work and fix defects.

Key points:

- **The contract sets the terms.** The retention percentage, any overall limit, and the release conditions are all in the contract. There is no single standard figure, so read the clauses.
- **It is usually released in stages.** A common arrangement is that part is released at completion and the remainder at the end of the defects liability period, but follow what your contract says.
- **It flows down.** Main contractors usually hold retention from subcontractors on similar terms.
- **It is easy to lose track of.** Retention can be held for a long time after a project finishes. If nobody tracks release dates, the money may never be claimed.

Track retention per project: total held, amount released, release conditions, and the date or event that triggers each release.

## The SOP Act process in general terms

The SOP Act gives parties to construction contracts in Singapore a statutory route to payment. In outline:

1. **Payment claim.** The claimant (for example, a subcontractor) serves a payment claim on the respondent (for example, the main contractor) for the amount it says is due.
2. **Payment response.** The respondent provides a payment response stating how much it will pay and, if less than claimed, the reasons for withholding.
3. **Adjudication.** If the claim is disputed or not paid, the claimant may apply for adjudication, a fast process where an adjudicator decides the amount payable.
4. **Payment.** The adjudicated amount becomes payable, subject to the procedures in the Act.

The Act sets specific timelines for each step and specific requirements for what a claim and response must contain. These details matter, and missing a deadline can affect your rights. Do not rely on memory or an old template: check the Act and BCA's guidance for the current position, and take advice where needed.

Points to understand when reading the Act:

- Which contracts it applies to, and which are excluded
- When a payment claim can be served, and what it must state
- What happens if no payment response is provided
- Which reasons for withholding can be raised later in adjudication
- How and when to apply for adjudication
- How the contract's payment terms interact with the Act

## Progress claim checklist

- [ ] Claim period and claim number correct
- [ ] Prepared on a cumulative basis from the priced BOQ
- [ ] Measured quantities or percentages supported by records
- [ ] Only approved or instructed variations included, clearly separated
- [ ] Materials on site included only if the contract allows
- [ ] Retention calculated at the contract percentage and limit
- [ ] Previously certified amounts match the last certificate
- [ ] GST applied at the prevailing rate where registered
- [ ] Claim content and timing checked against the contract and the SOP Act
- [ ] Served on the right party, in the right way, with proof of service kept
- [ ] Certificate or payment response recorded when received
- [ ] Differences between claimed and certified amounts followed up

## Linking claims to cost

A claim tells you what you have earned. It does not tell you whether the project is making money. Compare claimed value with cost to date for each workhead:

- If claimed value is running well ahead of cost, check you are not over-claiming in a way that will reverse.
- If cost is running ahead of claimed value, either you are under-claiming or the job is losing margin.

## Common mistakes

1. **Using an old template.** Requirements under the SOP Act and your contract may have changed. Check current requirements before you rely on a template.
2. **Claiming without backup.** Unsupported claims are cut. Keep measurement records and photos for every period.
3. **Mixing variations into contract work.** Show variations separately so they can be checked and certified on their own.
4. **Wrong retention.** Applying the wrong percentage, ignoring the limit, or forgetting to stop deducting once the limit is reached.
5. **Not tracking retention release.** Money held for a long time is easy to forget.
6. **Missing deadlines.** Late claims or late responses can affect your position. Track every date.
7. **Claims disconnected from cost.** Claiming without comparing to cost hides losing projects.
