A retention sum is money held back from each progress payment as security that the contractor will finish the work and fix any defects. In Singapore, the contract sets how much is held, the overall limit, and when it is released. A common arrangement is that the first half (the first moiety) is released at completion and the balance after the defects liability period. Retention money is easily forgotten, because it is often released long after the project has finished.

This guide explains how retention is calculated, how release works, and how to make sure you actually collect it. ## What retention is for

Retention protects the paying party against two risks:

1. **The contractor does not finish.** The money held helps cover the cost of completing the work.
2. **Defects appear after handover.** The money held helps cover the cost of fixing defects the contractor does not put right.

It applies at every level of the chain. The owner holds retention from the main contractor, and the main contractor usually holds retention from its subcontractors on similar terms.

## How retention is calculated

There is no single retention rate fixed by law in Singapore. The contract, or the standard form it is based on, sets:

- **The rate.** A percentage deducted from the value of each progress payment.
- **The limit.** A cap on the total held, usually expressed as a percentage of the contract sum.
- **What it applies to.** Whether retention is taken on variations and materials on site as well as contract work.
- **Release stages.** When and in what parts it is returned.
- **Alternatives.** Whether a banker's guarantee or other security can be given instead of cash retention.

Read these clauses at tender stage. They affect your cash flow for the whole job, and in some cases for a year or more after it. If you are a subcontractor, compare the subcontract retention terms with the main contract. A subcontract that holds a higher rate, or releases later than the main contract, leaves you funding the difference.

### Worked example

The figures below are illustrative only. Assume a contract sum of S$2,000,000, retention at 10% of each payment, and a limit of 5% of the contract sum.

| Point in the project | Gross value to date (S$) | Retention at 10% (S$) | Retention held (S$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim 3 | 400,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 |
| Claim 6 | 900,000 | 90,000 | 90,000 |
| Claim 7 | 1,100,000 | 110,000 | 100,000 (limit reached) |
| Final claim | 2,000,000 | 200,000 | 100,000 |

Once the limit is reached, retention stops increasing. From claim 7 onwards, the full value of new work is paid, less previous certificates. A common error is to keep deducting the percentage after the limit has been reached.

## When retention money is released

A common arrangement in Singapore contracts is release in two parts:

| Stage | Typical trigger | Typical amount |
|---|---|---|
| First moiety | Completion, often evidenced by a completion certificate | Half of the retention held |
| Second moiety | End of the defects liability period, once defects are made good, often evidenced by a maintenance or final certificate | The balance |

Your contract may differ. Some contracts release retention section by section, some tie the second release to a final account, and some allow a banker's guarantee to replace cash retention. Follow what your contract says, not what the last project did.

### What usually holds up release

1. **No completion certificate.** If completion is not formally certified, the first release may not be triggered.
2. **Outstanding defects.** The second release normally depends on defects being made good.
3. **Missing documents.** As-built drawings, operation and maintenance manuals, warranties and test certificates are often conditions of handover.
4. **Final account not agreed.** Some contracts link the last release to the final account.
5. **Nobody asks.** Retention is not always released automatically. If your contract requires a claim or application, make it.

## Retention and the SOP Act

The SOP Act gives a statutory right to progress payments. BCA's guidance says it covers claims made after the completion date, including final payment claims. Whether a particular retention amount can be pursued through an SOP payment claim depends on your contract terms, so take advice on specific cases.

One point to watch: since the 2019 amendments, BCA says a payment claim generally cannot be made more than 30 months after the work it relates to was last carried out. For construction contracts, BCA's notes on the amendments explain that this runs from the latest of certain events, including the last certificate of completion. Where a long defects liability period applies, check how this limit affects your retention claims. For the payment claim and response process itself, see our [SOP Act guide](/resources/sop-act-payment-claims-singapore).

## GST on retention

IRAS's guidance for the construction sector says GST on a retention sum is accounted for at the earlier of receiving payment or issuing an invoice, in the same way as progress payments. In practice this is usually when the retention is released and invoiced, not when it is first withheld. The GST rate is currently 9%. Confirm the treatment with your accountant and check [IRAS's construction page](https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/goods-services-tax-(gst)/specific-business-sectors/construction).

## Retention you hold from subcontractors

Main contractors are on both sides. Track retention held from subcontractors with the same care as retention held from you:

- Apply the rate and limit in each subcontract, not the main contract's
- Note release triggers, which may differ from the main contract
- Plan for the cash needed to release subcontractor retention, which may fall due before you receive your own

## How to track retention

For each project, keep a retention register with:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Rate and limit | From the contract |
| Retention held to date | Updated with every certified claim |
| Limit reached? | Date the limit was reached |
| Completion date | And the certificate reference |
| First release | Amount, due date, date received |
| Defects liability period | Start and end dates |
| Second release | Amount, due date, date received |
| Security in lieu | Banker's guarantee reference and expiry, if any |

Review the register monthly. Retention due for release should appear on your cash flow forecast alongside progress payments.

### A simple release routine

1. **At completion,** confirm the completion certificate has been issued and note its date.
2. **Within the same month,** apply for the first moiety if your contract requires an application, and record the due date.
3. **At the start of the defects liability period,** diarise its end date and a reminder some weeks before it.
4. **During the period,** log defects reported and the date each is closed, so you can show they were made good.
5. **At the end of the period,** request the maintenance or final certificate and apply for the balance.
6. **When paid,** record the amount and date received, and close the line in the register.

## Common mistakes

1. **Deducting past the limit.** Stop increasing retention once the cap is reached.
2. **Assuming release is automatic.** Check whether you must apply, and apply on time.
3. **Losing track after handover.** The project team moves on and the money is forgotten.
4. **Not recording the defects liability period.** Without the end date, nobody knows when the balance is due.
5. **Letting a banker's guarantee expire or overrun.** Track expiry dates as well as cash retention.
6. **Ignoring the 30-month limit.** Long delays can affect your right to claim under the Act.
7. **Mismatched subcontract terms.** Releasing subcontractor retention on main contract terms, or the reverse.
