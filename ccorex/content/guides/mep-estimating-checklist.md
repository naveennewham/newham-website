An MEP estimating checklist is a fixed list of checks your team runs on every tender: at receipt, after take-off, after pricing and before submission. It exists because most tender errors are not hard problems. They are missed addenda, an area nobody measured, a rate copied from an old job, or a client format that was broken on the way out. A checklist catches these consistently, whoever is estimating.

This guide gives a stage-by-stage MEP tender checklist you can adapt for fire protection, ACMV, ELV and A&A works, plus the mistakes it is designed to prevent.

## Why MEP tenders need a checklist

MEP estimating has more moving parts than most trades. Scope boundaries with the main contractor and other services are often unclear. Drawings change through addenda. Material prices move between tenders. Several people may touch one estimate, and the deadline rarely moves.

Without a checklist, quality depends on who happened to do the tender. With one, the team has a shared definition of "ready to submit", and reviewers know exactly what they are signing off.

A good checklist is:

- **Staged.** Checks sit at the point where the problem can still be fixed cheaply.
- **Short enough to use.** If it takes longer to fill in than to do the work, people skip it.
- **Owned.** Each item has a person responsible, and sign-off is recorded.
- **Trade-aware.** Common checks apply to every tender; trade-specific checks are added on top.

## The tender stages at a glance

| Stage | Main question | Typical owner | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Tender receipt | Should we bid, and what is in scope? | Estimating manager | Bid decision, tender summary |
| 2. Scope and documents | Do we understand the full scope? | Estimator | Scope notes, RFIs, exclusions list |
| 3. Area schedule and take-off | Is every area measured? | Estimator | Area schedule, material take-off |
| 4. Pricing | Is every line priced from a current source? | Estimator | Priced take-off |
| 5. Markups and summary | Are margins and adjustments approved? | Manager / director | Tender summary |
| 6. Review and approval | Has someone else checked it? | Reviewer | Signed-off estimate |
| 7. Submission | Is it in the client's format, complete and on time? | Estimator / admin | Submitted tender |

## Stage 1: Tender receipt

Before anyone starts measuring, decide whether to bid and record the basics.

- [ ] Tender logged with client, project name, closing date and time
- [ ] Bid or no-bid decision made and recorded
- [ ] Tender type identified: BOQ, Design & Build or hybrid
- [ ] Trades in scope listed (fire protection, ACMV, ELV, A&A works)
- [ ] Estimator assigned and internal deadlines set before the closing date
- [ ] Site visit or tender briefing dates noted

A one-page tender summary at this stage (scope, key dates, special conditions, submission requirements) saves the team from searching the documents again later.

## Stage 2: Scope and documents

Most expensive tender mistakes start here.

- [ ] Every document in the tender list received and opened
- [ ] All addenda and clarifications logged and read
- [ ] Specification read for each trade in scope
- [ ] Scope boundaries with other trades confirmed (power supply to equipment, builder's works, openings, plinths, ceiling access)
- [ ] Approved brands and "or equivalent" requirements noted
- [ ] Testing, commissioning and authority submission obligations identified
- [ ] Method of measurement noted, if the tender specifies one
- [ ] Conditions of contract checked for retention, defects liability, liquidated damages and payment terms
- [ ] Questions raised as RFIs before the query deadline
- [ ] Draft exclusions and qualifications list started

If the tender is a BOQ tender, also check the client's BOQ against the drawings. Billed quantities can be wrong and items can be missing. Our guide on [what a BOQ is](/resources/what-is-a-boq) explains the sections you should expect to find.

## Stage 3: Area schedule and take-off

Measure area by area so nothing is skipped.

- [ ] Area schedule set up by building, floor and room or zone
- [ ] Hazard classification recorded for each area (fire protection)
- [ ] Room use and special conditions noted for each area (ACMV)
- [ ] Every area in the schedule has quantities against it
- [ ] Take-off done from the latest drawing revisions
- [ ] Units consistent across similar items (m, nr, set, lot)
- [ ] Fittings, supports and accessories either measured or included in rates, consistently
- [ ] Equipment schedules cross-checked against layout drawings
- [ ] Quantities spot-checked by a second person on a sample of areas

For more on measurement approaches, see the [quantity takeoff methods guide](/resources/quantity-takeoff-methods).

## Stage 4: Pricing

Every line should be priced from a source you can name.

- [ ] Each item priced from the component library or a current supplier quote
- [ ] Material and installation prices kept separate
- [ ] Prices checked for date; old prices refreshed or flagged
- [ ] Supplier quotes compared on the same scope and specification
- [ ] Assemblies used for repeated combinations (for example, a sprinkler head with its drop and fittings) so they are priced the same way each time
- [ ] Subcontract packages priced with written quotes where possible
- [ ] Preliminaries priced: supervision, testing and commissioning, authority submissions, as-built drawings, warranties
- [ ] Provisional sums and prime cost items entered exactly as instructed

## Stage 5: Markups and summary

- [ ] Material and installation markups applied at the approved levels
- [ ] Contingency or risk allowance agreed and recorded, if used
- [ ] Discounts, if any, approved and shown as the tender requires
- [ ] Summary rolls up correctly from sections and sub-sections
- [ ] GST shown at the prevailing rate where the tender asks for it
- [ ] Exclusions and qualifications finalised

## Stage 6: Review and approval

- [ ] Reviewed by someone other than the estimator
- [ ] Validation checks run for each trade (for example, every area has items, no zero-rate lines, units match item types)
- [ ] Arithmetic checked: line amounts, subtotals, summary
- [ ] Large or unusual items explained in notes
- [ ] Pricing approved by the estimating manager
- [ ] Final margin approved by the person authorised to do so
- [ ] Approval recorded with name and date

## Stage 7: Submission

- [ ] Prices carried onto the client's BOQ or form without renumbering or adding items
- [ ] Client's quantities left unchanged unless the tender allows changes; differences qualified in writing
- [ ] Required forms, schedules and technical submittals completed
- [ ] Exports produced in the required format (Excel, PDF or both)
- [ ] File names and document versions checked
- [ ] Submitted before the closing time, with proof of submission kept
- [ ] Final submitted version archived so it can be found at award

## Trade-specific checks

Add these to the common checklist for each trade.

### Fire protection

- [ ] Hazard class per area agrees with the specification
- [ ] Sprinkler, hydrant, hose reel, wet riser, dry riser and pump scope confirmed
- [ ] Fire alarm and interface scope confirmed with ELV and electrical
- [ ] Authority submission and testing requirements priced

See the [fire protection estimating guide](/resources/fire-protection-estimating) for more detail.

### ACMV

- [ ] Equipment selections match the schedules and specification
- [ ] Ductwork, insulation and diffusers measured for every area
- [ ] Controls and BMS interfaces scoped
- [ ] Testing, balancing and commissioning priced

### ELV

- [ ] Systems in scope listed (CCTV, access control, PA, structured cabling and others named in the tender)
- [ ] Containment responsibility confirmed
- [ ] Head-end equipment, licences and programming included where required

### A&A works

- [ ] Existing conditions checked on site
- [ ] Dismantling, temporary works and making good priced
- [ ] Working hour restrictions and access constraints reflected

## Common mistakes

1. **Checking only at the end.** By the night before closing there is no time to re-measure an area. Put checks where problems can still be fixed.
2. **Missing an addendum.** A late addendum that changes a drawing or adds scope is easy to overlook. Log every one.
3. **Unmeasured areas.** Without an area schedule, a whole floor or plant room can be left out.
4. **Undated rates.** A rate with no date and no source cannot be defended or trusted.
5. **Inconsistent units.** Including fittings in one section and measuring them separately in another causes double counting or gaps.
6. **Self-review only.** The person who built the estimate is the least likely to spot its errors.
7. **Breaking the client's format.** Renumbering or adding items makes your tender hard to compare.
8. **No record of approval.** If nobody can say who approved the margin, nobody owns it.
