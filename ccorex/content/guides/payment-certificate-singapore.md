A payment certificate is the document that states how much a contractor is to be paid for a period of work. In Singapore construction, an interim (or progress) payment certificate is usually issued each month in answer to the contractor's progress claim. It shows the gross valuation of work done to date, deducts retention and amounts already certified, and states the amount now due. At the end of the job, a final certificate settles the balance.

This guide explains what a payment certificate contains, who issues it under common Singapore contract arrangements, how it fits with the Security of Payment Act (SOP Act), how GST works on certified amounts, and the mistakes that cost contractors money. It is general guidance, not legal advice. Your contract decides the details, so read its payment terms alongside this guide.

## Claim, certificate, invoice: the three documents

A monthly payment cycle in Singapore usually involves three documents:

| Document | Issued by | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Progress claim (payment claim) | Contractor or subcontractor | States what the claimant says it is owed for work done to date |
| Payment certificate (or payment response) | The certifier named in the contract, or the paying party | States how much will be paid, and why any amount is withheld |
| Tax invoice | Contractor, if GST-registered | Bills the certified amount with GST |

The claim comes first and the certificate answers it. IRAS's GST guide for the construction industry describes the same sequence: the contractor issues a letter of claim, the customer's architect values and certifies the work done, and the certified amount may differ from the claim. For how to build up the claim itself, see our [progress claims guide](/resources/progress-claims-singapore).

## What an interim payment certificate contains

Certificate layouts vary by contract and by certifier, but most interim certificates are built on a cumulative basis, the same way as a progress claim. The typical lines are:

| Line | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Gross valuation to date | Value of contract work done to date, plus approved variations, plus materials on site if the contract allows |
| Less retention | Retention at the contract rate, up to any limit set in the contract |
| Net valuation to date | Gross valuation less retention |
| Less previously certified | Total of all earlier certificates |
| Amount due this certificate | Net valuation less previously certified |
| GST | At the prevailing rate (currently 9%), where it is shown on the certificate |
| Total payable | Amount due plus GST |

A certificate will also usually identify the contract, the certificate number, the period or valuation date, and the claim it relates to. Some certificates attach a valuation summary by trade or BOQ section, so the contractor can see exactly which items were reduced.

### Why cumulative matters

Because each certificate values everything done to date and then subtracts what has already been certified, any under-valuation in one month can be corrected in the next. It also means a single wrong figure, such as an incorrect "previously certified" total, carries forward into every later certificate until someone spots it.

### Retention on the certificate

Retention is held back from each certificate as security for completion and defects. The rate, the limit and the release stages are set by the contract. Check that the certifier stops increasing retention once the limit is reached, and that releases appear on the certificate when they fall due. Our [retention sum guide](/resources/retention-sum-singapore) covers the calculation and release in detail.

## Who issues payment certificates

The certifier is whoever your contract names. BCA's SOP Act Information Kit explains the usual arrangement:

- **Public sector main contracts.** The agency usually administers the contract through a Superintending Officer (SO), who can be an officer of the agency or an external consultant. The SO can certify payment to the contractor. Public sector projects commonly use the Public Sector Standard Conditions of Contract (PSSCOC), published by [BCA](https://www1.bca.gov.sg/growth-and-transformation/procurement/standard-contract-forms/public-sector-standard-conditions-of-contract-psscoc/).
- **Private sector projects.** The Architect may take on the certifying role. Many private projects use a Singapore Institute of Architects (SIA) form or a bespoke contract.
- **Subcontracts and below.** BCA notes there is usually no such certifier arrangement. The main contractor values and responds to the subcontractor's claim itself.

In practice, a quantity surveyor often prepares the valuation that the certifier signs, and some contracts use an employer's representative or engineer instead. Do not assume the arrangement from a previous project. Read your contract's payment clauses to find out who certifies, when certificates must be issued, what they must show, and when payment falls due after the certificate.

## Payment certificates and the SOP Act

Under the Building and Construction Industry Security of Payment Act, the claimant serves a payment claim and the respondent must provide a payment response. For construction contracts, BCA says a payment response is required in all circumstances, even when the respondent intends to pay in full.

BCA's Information Kit states that a payment response "includes payment certificates issued by the Superintending Officer or Architect provided such certificates meet the requirements of a payment response as defined in the Act". In other words, a certificate can do double duty as the statutory payment response, but only if it meets those requirements. According to BCA, a payment response must:

1. Be in writing and addressed to the claimant
2. Identify the payment claim it relates to
3. State the amount the respondent proposes to pay, or "nil" if it proposes to pay nothing
4. Include all supporting reasons where the amount is less than the claim, or payment is withheld
5. Include a reasonable breakdown of the items for each reason
6. Include calculations showing how the response amount is derived
7. Include any other applicable documents

It must also be given on time. BCA says the response is due by the date in the contract, subject to a maximum of 21 days after the date the payment claim is required to be served, or 14 days if the contract says nothing.

This matters for both sides:

- **For certifiers and respondents.** A certificate that simply states a lower figure, with no reasons or breakdown, may not meet the requirements. For construction contracts, reasons for withholding that are not in the payment response generally cannot be raised later in adjudication.
- **For contractors.** Read every certificate as if it were the payment response. If the certified amount is lower than you claimed and you do not accept it, the SOP Act timelines for disputing it and applying for adjudication start running.

The Information Kit also notes that an adjudicator is not bound by the payment response, or by any assessment the contract says is final or binding. For the full process, deadlines and adjudication, see our [SOP Act payment claims guide](/resources/sop-act-payment-claims-singapore) and [BCA's Security of Payment page](https://www1.bca.gov.sg/regulatory-info/security-of-payment).

## GST on certified amounts

GST at the prevailing rate, currently 9%, applies to the value of work certified if the contractor is GST-registered. IRAS's e-Tax guide on the construction industry sets out three points worth knowing:

- **A letter of claim is not a tax invoice.** It does not contain the final amount payable, because the certified amount may differ.
- **Invoice on certification.** The contractor should issue a tax invoice once the architect has certified the agreed amount, and keep the certificate of work done with the tax invoice to support the value invoiced.
- **Time of supply.** For progress payments, GST is accounted for at the earlier of receiving payment or issuing an invoice. The same rule applies to retention when it is released and invoiced.

If a certificate is later corrected, IRAS says to issue a credit note or an additional tax invoice to reflect the adjustment. See [IRAS's construction sector page](https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/goods-services-tax-(gst)/specific-business-sectors/construction) and confirm the treatment with your accountant.

Some certificates show GST as a line; others certify a net amount and leave GST to the contractor's tax invoice. Either way, the tax invoice is the contractor's document.

## The final certificate

Once the works are complete, the defects liability period has ended and the final account is agreed, the certifier usually issues a final certificate. It states the final contract sum, including all variations and adjustments, less everything previously paid, and certifies the balance due to either party. The remaining retention is often released around this point.

What a final certificate does in law depends on your contract. Some contracts give it a degree of finality on certain matters, and there may be deadlines for challenging it. Read those clauses carefully before you accept a final certificate, and take advice if the figures are disputed.

The SOP Act still applies at the end of a project. BCA's Information Kit says progress payment claims made after completion, including claims for final payments where applicable, are covered by the Act. Watch the time limit for serving claims, which BCA explains in its notes on the 2019 amendments.

## Worked example

The figures are illustrative only. Assume a contract sum of S$1,200,000, retention at 10% of each certificate, limited to 5% of the contract sum (S$60,000). The contractor is GST-registered. Interim certificate No. 4:

| Line | S$ |
|---|---|
| Contract work done to date | 480,000 |
| Approved variations to date | 30,000 |
| Materials on site | 20,000 |
| **Gross valuation to date** | **530,000** |
| Less retention at 10% (limit S$60,000 not yet reached) | (53,000) |
| **Net valuation to date** | **477,000** |
| Less previously certified (certificates 1 to 3) | (351,000) |
| **Amount due this certificate** | **126,000** |
| GST at 9% | 11,340 |
| **Total payable** | **137,340** |

Two checks a contractor should make before issuing the tax invoice:

1. **Previously certified.** Certificate No. 3 had a gross valuation of S$390,000 and retention of S$39,000, giving a net of S$351,000. That matches the figure deducted here.
2. **Retention limit.** Retention to date is S$53,000, below the S$60,000 limit. On the next certificate, retention can only rise by S$7,000 more, however much work is valued.

If the contractor had claimed S$560,000 gross and the certificate values S$530,000, the certificate should show which items were reduced and why. If it does not, ask, and check whether it meets the requirements of a payment response.

## Common mistakes

1. **Treating the certificate as a formality.** It may be the statutory payment response. Read it on the day it arrives and diarise the SOP Act deadlines.
2. **Not reconciling to the claim.** Compare the certificate line by line with your claim, and record every difference and the reason given.
3. **Wrong "previously certified" figure.** Check it against the last certificate, not the last claim or the last payment received.
4. **Retention past the limit.** Make sure deductions stop once the cap is reached, and that releases are certified when due.
5. **Invoicing the claim instead of the certificate.** IRAS says the tax invoice should follow certification. Invoicing the claimed amount leads to credit notes and GST corrections.
6. **Losing the certificate.** Keep each certificate with its tax invoice, as IRAS expects.
7. **Assuming last project's contract.** Who certifies, and when, is set by each contract. Check every time.
8. **Accepting a final certificate without reading the finality terms.** Your contract may limit what can be challenged later.
